A.1 · Shoot the Pictures

Three Overlapping Image Sequences

I used my phone’s fixed 1× camera setting for every sequence. I stayed at one shooting position and rotated the camera between views, aiming to keep its center of projection (COP) fixed. Keeping the zoom unchanged and rotating about the same viewpoint makes a homography a useful model for the shared scene.

I aimed for the assignment’s recommended 40%–70% overlap, retaining common window corners, roof edges, columns, and entrances in neighboring photographs. These are handheld sequences, so small COP changes and movement in vegetation remain possible sources of error.

Before processing, I apply EXIF orientation, convert to RGB, and resize proportionally to a maximum side of 1200 pixels. All landscape views below are processed at 1200 × 900 pixels; the original files remain unchanged.

Estimate a Projective Transformation

From Point Pairs to a Linear System

A homography maps source coordinates (x, y) to target coordinates (u, v) up to homogeneous scale. Fixing its bottom-right entry to 1 leaves eight unknown parameters.

λ[uv1]=[h11h12h13h21h22h23h31h321][xy1]
[xy1000−ux−uy000xy1−vx−vy]𝐡=[uv]

Stacking all 12 pairs produces a 24 × 8 system. I solve the overdetermined least-squares problem directly with a linear-system solver, then reshape the eight parameters into the homography. No high-level homography estimator is used.

A.3 · Warp the Images

Inverse Warping and Image Rectification

For each destination pixel, I apply the inverse homography and divide by the homogeneous scale to find its source location. This fills the output by sampling the source, including when the canvas extends to negative reference coordinates.

[XYW]=H−1[uv1],(x,y)=(XW,YW)

Two Sampling Methods

Nearest neighbor selects the source pixel at the rounded coordinates. Bilinear interpolation combines four neighboring samples using the fractional distances from the upper-left sample. Both samplers are implemented directly; neither uses a library warp or interpolation routine.

INN(x,y)=I(round(x),round(y))
x0=⌊x⌋,y0=⌊y⌋,α=x−x0,β=y−y0
Ibil=(1−α)(1−β)I00+α(1−β)I10+(1−α)βI01+αβI11

Here I₀₀ = I(x₀, y₀), I₁₀ = I(x₀ + 1, y₀), I₀₁ = I(x₀, y₀ + 1), and I₁₁ = I(x₀ + 1, y₀ + 1). At the last source row or column, neighbor indices are clamped to the image boundary. Samples outside the valid image region contribute no color.

Rectify Two A4 Sheets

I map the four corners of each paper, ordered top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left, to (0, 0), (700, 0), (700, 990), and (0, 990). The coordinate spans preserve the known A4 ratio 210:297 = 70:99. Including both endpoint pixel centers gives a raster of 701 × 991 pixels.

A.4 · Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Register, Warp, and Feather

For each scene, I estimate homographies between the annotated neighboring views and compose them into a common reference coordinate system. The middle view is the reference: photograph 02 for the first two scenes and photograph 03 for the five-view courtyard sequence.

Hr→r=I3,Hi→r=Hj→rHi→j

I transform each image’s four corners and take the floor of the coordinate minima and the ceiling of the maxima to construct a shared canvas. Its offset expresses output pixels in the reference coordinate system. Each image and its weight map are inverse-warped onto this canvas using bilinear sampling.

The source weight increases with distance from the nearest boundary. For a source image of width W and height H, I normalize the distance map before warping it:

d(x,y)=min{x+1,W−x,y+1,H−y},w=dmaxd
M(q)=∑iwi(q)Ii(q)∑iwi(q),∑iwi(q)>0

Invalid samples have zero weight; a legitimate black pixel inside an image remains valid. For comparison, the overwrite baseline places the same warped images on the same canvas but lets later views replace earlier ones.

Results. The overwrite comparisons expose abrupt brightness boundaries in the sky, road, and steps. Feathering reduces these seams, and the main building façades in the first two mosaics form continuous structures. The courtyard mosaic combines all five views into a much wider scene.

Some fine railings and vegetation remain soft or show small double edges. Blending cannot correct geometric misalignment, possible parallax, or scene movement. The wide planar courtyard mosaic also stretches its peripheral regions substantially. Black outer regions are parts of the bounding canvas that receive no valid source samples.

A.5 · Nonplanar Mapping · CS280A

A Cylindrical View of the Courtyard

I construct a cylindrical mosaic from the same five courtyard photographs, reusing their source-to-reference homographies. The reference remains 03.jpg. This changes the projection surface while preserving the underlying correspondence data.

Cylindrical Coordinates

Let (x, y) be a point in the reference image plane, (cₓ, cᵧ) the reference image center, and f the focal parameter in pixels at the working resolution. The forward mapping is:

θ=tan−1(x−cxf),u=fθ+cx,v=(y−cy)cosθ+cy

For inverse sampling, a cylindrical output pixel maps back to the reference plane as follows:

θ=u−cxf,x=ftanθ+cx,y=v−cycosθ+cy

Sample Directly from the Source Photographs

I compose the inverse cylindrical mapping with each inverse homography: an output pixel first maps to the reference plane, then to its location in a source photograph. I sample the working source image directly with bilinear interpolation and sample its feathering weight at the same location. The final colors are blended with the weighted average from A.4.

pi∼Hi→r−1C−1(qcyl)

This avoids resampling an already rendered planar panorama. To find the output canvas, I map the source-image boundaries to the cylinder and include a one-pixel margin around their combined extent.

Projection comparison. All three cylindrical results cover the courtyard and surrounding buildings. Compared with the planar baseline, the outer scene is substantially less elongated. Rooflines, railings, and the outer boundaries change shape because a different projection surface is being used.

The cylinder does not remove the remaining local registration errors. Fine structures can still be soft or doubled after feathering, and black outer regions still indicate uncovered canvas. The selected 900 px result is a qualitative choice at this working resolution.

Reflection & Sources

What I Learned

Homography estimation turns a set of matching features into a shared coordinate system, while inverse warping determines how that geometry becomes an image. Implementing both samplers made the tradeoff between sharp pixel steps, smoothness, and computation visible in the rectified poster.

The panorama experiments also separate two sources of artifacts. Feathering improves brightness transitions but leaves geometric errors behind; choosing a cylindrical surface reduces wide-view planar stretching but does not correct parallax. Better acquisition and more accurate registration remain important even with smoother blending.
